# pseudo-jepa
<!-- id: pseudo-jepa · chapter: Overview · scene: overview · map: all -->

> **v1 is a CLIP with a predictor bolted on, not a real JEPA yet.** It is trained, and it runs in your browser on the Results tab. The v2 tab is the JEPA I have not trained yet.

Give it a photo and a sentence. It turns each one into a list of 256 numbers, and the closer the two lists point the same way, the more the sentence describes the photo.

Everything is built from scratch in PyTorch, block by block, out of its basic layers (`Conv2d`, `Linear`, `LayerNorm`), and trained from random weights. The left panel follows one real photo and one real caption through the network. The drawing in the corner is my sketch of it; the yellow box marks where we are.

## One photo, five captions
<!-- id: data · chapter: Data · scene: data · map: data -->

Flickr30k: 31,014 photos, each described by five different people. I train on 29,000 and keep 1,014 for checking. Captions are not shared between photos, so every photo has its own five.

Each training step picks one of the five at random. Same photo, slightly different wording every time it comes around.

## What the network sees
<!-- id: input · scene: input · map: image -->

The photo is shrunk so its short side is 128 pixels, then cropped to the centre square: $128 \times 128 \times 3$ numbers. While training, the crop is also random, flipped and colour-jittered, so the network can't memorise exact pixels.

## Convolution Stem
<!-- id: conv · chapter: Image Encoder · scene: conv · map: conv -->

Four blocks, each `Conv2D(k=3, s=2, p=1)` → `GroupNorm(8)` → `GELU` (the last block skips the GELU). A 3 × 3 window slides over the image two pixels at a time and writes one number per stop, per channel. Every block halves the width and height:

$$128^2 \times 3 \;\to\; 64^2 \times 64 \;\to\; 32^2 \times 128 \;\to\; 16^2 \times 256 \;\to\; 8^2 \times 256$$

The panels on the left are real channels from this photo. Early ones respond to edges and colour; by the last block each cell sums up a 16 × 16 pixel patch.

## Cutting into tokens
<!-- id: tokens · scene: tokens · map: pos -->

The $8 \times 8 \times 256$ output is read cell by cell: **64 tokens**, each a vector of 256 numbers. Each strip on the left is one token; blue is positive, purple negative.

The tokens don't know where they sat in the grid, so each one gets a learned **Positional Embedding** added: one 256-number vector per position, trained like any other weight.

## Transformer Stem
<!-- id: image-blocks · scene: image-blocks · map: image-blocks -->

Six identical blocks. In each one, every token looks at every other token (**attention**), takes a weighted average of what it sees, then passes through a small two-layer network on its own. Both steps are added back onto the token, so a block can only nudge it, never replace it.

The heat over the photo is real attention from this photo: where the highlighted token is looking, block by block.

A final **LayerNorm** rescales each token. Out come the visual tokens: $64 \times 256$.

## Words to vectors
<!-- id: embed · chapter: Text Encoder · scene: embed · map: embed -->

The caption is lowercased and split into words. Every word that appears at least five times in the training captions gets a number: 7,441 of them. Anything else becomes `<unk>`.

The **Embedding Matrix** has one row per word, 256 numbers wide. A word's vector is simply its row. Like the image side, each position then gets its own **Positional Embedding**.

## Text Transformer Stem
<!-- id: text-blocks · scene: text-blocks · map: text-blocks -->

Six more blocks, the same code as the image side. Captions in a batch have different lengths, so short ones are padded, and the attention is **masked** so no word ever looks at padding.

The arcs show which word each word attends to, in this caption.

## One vector per caption: the TARGET
<!-- id: target · scene: target · map: target -->

After the last LayerNorm there is one vector per word. Average them (real words only), then scale the result to length 1. That single 256-number vector is the **TARGET**: what the image side is trying to land on.

## The Predictor and its accumulator q
<!-- id: predictor · chapter: Predictor · scene: predictor · map: predictor -->

The predictor takes the 64 visual tokens through a `Linear(256, 256)`, then appends one extra token, **q**. q is a learned parameter, the same for every photo, and starts as small random numbers.

Two Transformer blocks later, q has attended to all 64 tokens and soaked up a summary of the photo. Only q is kept: `LayerNorm` → `Linear(256, 256)` → scaled to length 1. The heat shows where q looked.

That vector is the image side's guess, its **VISUAL TOKENS** $[B, 1, 256]$.

## The score
<!-- id: score · chapter: Loss · scene: score · map: score -->

Both vectors have length 1, so their dot product is the cosine of the angle between them: 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated.

$$\text{score} = \hat y \cdot y = \sum_{i=1}^{256} \hat y_i \, y_i$$

## Loss: InfoNCE
<!-- id: infonce · scene: infonce · map: loss -->

A batch holds 256 photos and their 256 captions. Score every photo against every caption and you get $S$, a $256 \times 256$ table (8 × 8 on the left). The diagonal holds the true pairs and **should be HIGHEST**; everything else **should be low**.

- **Each row** is a multiple-choice question: which caption belongs to this photo? Softmax the row, take $-\log p$ of the right answer. Averaged over rows: `loss_i`.
- **Each column** asks the reverse, which photo belongs to this caption: `loss_t`.

$$\text{Loss} = \frac{\text{loss}_i + \text{loss}_t}{2}$$

Before the softmax, $S$ is multiplied by a learned scale $e^{t}$, starting at $1/0.07 \approx 14$. A bigger scale makes the softmax pickier.

## Training
<!-- id: training · chapter: Training · scene: training · map: loss -->

200 epochs, batch 256, AdamW at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, one epoch of warm-up, then cosine decay. At $5 \times 10^{-4}$ without warm-up, both encoders collapsed to the same output for everything. On my Mac's GPU, a step takes about half a second; the run took 3.4 hours.

Guessing at random gives a validation loss of $\ln 1014 \approx 6.92$. The best was **4.67** at epoch 59, and that checkpoint is the one on the Results tab. After that, training loss kept falling while validation loss climbed: 29,000 photos are not enough for 14 million weights trained from scratch.

## Why "pseudo"
<!-- id: pseudo · chapter: Next · scene: pseudo · map: all -->

A JEPA predicts the *representation* of something hidden, and grades the guess against a slowly-updated copy of an encoder, with no need for wrong answers. v1 does neither: nothing is hidden from the photo, both encoders learn directly from the same loss, and it only works because every batch has 255 wrong captions to push away. That is CLIP's recipe, plus a predictor.

The **v2** tab sketches the real thing: hide parts of the photo and of the caption, and predict a slow copy's view of what is missing.
